Can AI read a battery's degradation plots?

A benchmark, and a negative result. Seven open vision-language models looked at diagnostic plots of ageing lithium-ion cells and tried to name the damage. None did better than guessing, and every one of them sounded sure.

Research · preprint in preparation

The question

A battery loses capacity in different ways: it can lose lithium, or lose active material at either electrode. Experts tell these apart from the shape of two curves, incremental capacity (dQ/dV) and differential voltage (dV/dQ). It takes training, and classifiers trained on one chemistry fall apart on another.

The signatures are described in thousands of papers, and language models have read many of them. So I wondered whether a general-purpose vision-language model could connect the peaks it sees to that literature and infer the diagnosis, without training for the task. Open models matter here because they can run locally when cell data cannot leave the lab.

How I tested it

  • 200 labelled plots from published synthetic data (Dubarry and Beck): 8 chemistries, 5 degradation modes, 5 severities from 3 to 30%.
  • Seven input conditions that separate seeing from knowing: both plots, one plot only, the reference curve removed, five worked examples, a text description of the peaks instead of an image, and shuffled answer order.
  • Seven open models from 2 billion to 235 billion parameters (Qwen3-VL and Gemma 3), compared against a simple logistic regression on the raw curves.
  • 40 real cells (LG M50T), labelled by a published degradation analysis, as a check outside the simulator.

What happened

Five-way accuracy was 0.15 to 0.25, against a chance level of 0.20. After correcting for the many comparisons, none of the 50 model and condition combinations was significantly better than chance, and a hundredfold increase in model size did not help. The logistic regression scored 1.00 on the chemistry it trained on and 0.39 on unseen chemistries, so the task is learnable, but only the narrow way.

The more interesting part is how the models fail. They fixate on one or two labels: Qwen3-VL 8B answered "loss of active material, delithiated positive electrode" on all 200 plots, and Gemma 3 4B answered "loss of lithium inventory" on all 200. Shuffling the answer order showed this is a preference for certain labels, not for certain positions in the list.

And they are confidently wrong. Each bar pair below shows what the model said it was sure of next to how often it was right, for the plots with both curves shown. The dashed line is chance.

On the 40 real cells the picture repeated: no model beat the rate you get by always answering the most common label (0.55), while a regression on the curves reached 0.70.

Why I care

A model that is wrong is a problem. A model that is wrong and sure is a hazard, especially in safety work such as battery diagnostics. This is why Battlas keeps language models out of the number-making path: numbers and intervals come from calibrated statistics, and a language model, where there is one, only narrates. It is also the starting point for my next question: how well can an AI agent tell when a battery question is unanswerable, and can that be guaranteed?
